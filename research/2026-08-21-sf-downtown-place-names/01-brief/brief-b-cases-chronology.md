Task
Settle the five contested naming questions in San Francisco's downtown / South of Market district, and build the event chronology that explains them, 1847 to the present. The operating principle is that a neighborhood is not a timeless polygon but a historical claim made by a particular source, at a particular time, with a particular degree of geographic precision and social or administrative authority. This commission is depth, not breadth: a sibling run covers the full pilot gazetteer, so do not attempt an inventory of every name in the district — spend the room on the five cases and the chronology. CASE ONE, SoMa versus South of Market as distinct name events. South of Market is a broad directional-geographic term whose practical extent differs by period and source. SoMa is a separate name event: establish when it first appears in print, who put it there, whether it was coined in imitation of SoHo, whether its extent at first use matched South of Market's, and how its usage and extent moved afterward. Do not assume equivalence at any date, and do not treat the acronym's existence as evidence of a neighborhood's existence. CASE TWO, South of the Slot. Establish its date range, its register, and who used it about whom — the term refers to the Market Street cable conduit, and its social freight matters as much as its geography. Say plainly whether any official source ever adopted it, and if the answer is no, say that too. Address Jack London's 1909 story of that title as an attestation and assess whether it created, popularised, or merely recorded the term. CASE THREE, the 100 Vara District as survey grid versus social area. Establish its survey geometry from survey materials as precisely as the evidence allows, including its relationship to the 50 Vara Survey and to the O'Farrell survey, and then treat separately and sceptically the question of whether it ever carried a social or neighborhood meaning to the people living in it, as opposed to a property-grid meaning to the people transacting over it. Do not collapse it into SoMa. CASE FOUR, Rincon Hill across the 1869 Second Street Cut. Treat the Cut as a physical transformation event: establish who ordered it, why, what it did to the hill, and what it did to the value and reputation of what stood on it. Research pre-cut and post-cut usage and extent separately, and date rather than assert the transition between the name's four successive identities — topographic feature, elite residential district, industrial and working hinterland, and redevelopment-era neighborhood. Include the Rincon Hill / Rincon Point distinction if the sources support one. CASE FIVE, The East Cut. Document the adoption: who proposed it, through what body, on what timeline, with what stated boundaries, what stated historical reference to the Second Street Cut, and what public and press reaction — including objections from residents of the older names it overlays. Assess whether it has been adopted by anyone other than the body that coined it. Then, CHRONOLOGY: the physical, institutional, demographic and political events from 1847 to the present that changed what these names meant or where they reached, each dated, sourced, and stating which names it affected and how.

Context
Commissioned by DHK. This is one of two commissions drawn from the same project brief and the same attached extract. This one takes the five contested cases and the chronology at length. Its sibling produces the full pilot gazetteer — every name at even depth — so breadth is covered elsewhere and is not this run's job. The two runs are independent and neither sees the other's output; where their source registries overlap, the agreement or disagreement between them is itself a check on both. The five cases were singled out because they are where the district's naming history is genuinely contested or genuinely misreported, not merely undocumented — the errors they invite are named in the attached extract's Section 11. The downstream consumer is a version-controlled research corpus — place records, time-bounded name events, source-specific geometry events, evidence assertions, and a relationship graph — that will later feed a map with a time slider and name search. This commission covers research substance only. The repository scaffolding, JSON schemas and Git governance described in the fuller project brief are being built separately and have been deliberately stripped from the attached extract; do not produce a project plan, a repository layout, or advice on how to run the research programme. What is wanted is the material a human researcher would otherwise spend months assembling from the San Francisco History Center at SFPL, the David Rumsey Map Collection, Sanborn fire insurance volumes, city and business directories, historic block books, the Bancroft Library, the California Historical Society, San Francisco Planning's area plans and historic resource surveys, and the digitised newspaper record. Web search is enabled on this run specifically because the deliverable's whole value is provenance — a recalled citation to an 1850s directory that turns out not to exist is worse than no citation at all — so treat the difference between a source you fetched and read today and a source you merely remember as the most important distinction in your output.

Constraints
Depth here means evidentiary depth, not length. A case settled in four sourced paragraphs is finished; do not pad it. Do not manufacture exact start or end dates. Where evidence is partial, give a range and say what bounds it. The first-print-appearance questions — when SoMa appears, when South of the Slot appears — are the ones most likely to be answered with a confident wrong date; give the specific citation that fixes each earliest attestation, or say explicitly that you could not fix it and give the earliest you could actually verify. Do not back-project present-day neighborhood boundaries into earlier periods; if the only boundary you can find is a modern one, say so and give no historical extent. Do not give a colloquial or cultural name a crisp parcel-level boundary — match the precision of the claim to the precision of the evidence. Preserve disagreement rather than resolving it: where two credible sources place a name differently, report both with their evidence and say which is better supported and why, and never let a secondary or retrospective account silently override contrary contemporaneous evidence. Distinguish throughout between a source contemporaneous with the usage — an 1899 Sanborn sheet labelling an area — and a later account describing that usage — a 2020 article saying what people called it in 1899; both are usable, they are not the same class of evidence, and the difference must be visible per claim rather than stated once as a caveat. Treat real-estate and branding language as evidence of what a developer or a business improvement district wanted, never as neutral historical fact; this applies with particular force to Case Five, where the coining body's own materials are a primary source about its intentions and not a source about the district. Every citation must carry enough to retrieve it: title, creator or publisher, date, repository, and identifier or URL. Mark each citation explicitly as FETCHED — you retrieved and read it during this run, with the URL as it read today — or RECALLED — it comes from training and you did not verify it. Do not blur the two. If you could not verify something you believe to be true, say so and place it under gaps rather than dressing it as a finding.

Output needs
A document that stands alone for a reader who has not seen this brief, in this order. (1) THE FIVE CASES, in the task's order, each opening with a one-paragraph verdict stating what the evidence establishes and how firmly, before the supporting detail. Every dated claim carries its source and locator inline and its FETCHED or RECALLED marker. Where a case cannot be settled on available evidence, say so in the verdict paragraph rather than burying it — an unsettled case reported as unsettled is a result. (2) CHRONOLOGY, 1847 to present, as a dated table or dated entries covering at minimum: the 1847 O'Farrell survey and the Yerba Buena to San Francisco renaming; the vara survey grids; the Gold Rush build-out and Rincon Hill's residential period; the filling of Yerba Buena Cove and the moving shoreline; the 1869 Second Street Cut; the industrialisation of the area south of Market and the waterfront; the 1906 earthquake and fire and the rebuilding; the Bay Bridge and the Transbay Terminal; mid-century redevelopment and the Yerba Buena Center project; the Embarcadero Freeway and its removal after 1989; late-century SoMa usage and the East SoMa area plan; and the 21st-century Transbay build-out and branding era. Each entry: date, event, source, which names it affected, and how — an event that changed nothing about the names belongs out of the table, not in it with an empty column. (3) SOURCE REGISTRY: a table of every source you actually used, with title, creator, date, repository, identifier or stable URL, source class (primary cartographic, primary administrative, primary contemporaneous textual, secondary interpretive), FETCHED or RECALLED, and a one-line reliability note saying what that source can and cannot settle. (4) CONFLICT REGISTER: every place where sources disagree about a name's date, extent, or meaning, with both positions, their evidence, and your reading of which is stronger — and the ones you cannot adjudicate left explicitly unadjudicated. (5) GAPS AND NEXT MOVES: what a human researcher must go to a physical archive or a paywalled newspaper database to settle, named specifically enough to act on — which collection, which years, which map series, which newspaper and which date range — ranked by how much the answer to a case depends on it. (6) WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS ANSWER.
