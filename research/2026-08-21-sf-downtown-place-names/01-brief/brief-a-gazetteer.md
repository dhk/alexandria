Task
Build the gazetteer layer for a historical corpus of San Francisco place names, restricted to the downtown / South of Market pilot, 1847 to the present. The operating principle is that a neighborhood is not a timeless polygon but a historical claim made by a particular source, at a particular time, with a particular degree of geographic precision and social or administrative authority; treat every name accordingly. This commission is breadth, not depth: cover every name below at uniform depth. A separate commission handles the extended treatment of the five contested cases and the event chronology, so do not expand any single entry into an essay here — an even gazetteer is what this run is for. NAMES, each its own entry: Yerba Buena; Yerba Buena Gardens; San Francisco (the 1847 renaming event only); 100 Vara District / 100 Vara Survey; the 50 Vara Survey where it bounds the 100 Vara; South of Market; South of the Slot; SoMa / SOMA; East SoMa; Rincon Hill; Rincon; Rincon Point; Happy Valley; Pleasant Valley if attested alongside it; South Beach; South Park; Steamboat Point; Tar Flat; Transbay; Financial District; Embarcadero; The East Cut; Second Street Cut. For each, answer in this order: (1) every attested spelling, abbreviation and alias, transcribed as the source writes it, not normalised; (2) earliest and latest dated evidence of use, each tied to one specific source and locator — page, sheet, column, plate — never to a general impression that the name was "in use by" some decade; (3) who used it and in what register: surveyor, assessor, ordinance, newspaper, directory, insurance cartographer, resident, planner, developer, or later historian; (4) place class, using the closed set in the attached extract's controlled vocabulary and no invented labels; (5) the spatial extent that source actually supports at that moment, described in streets, blocks, shoreline or survey lines, with precision declared as exact, approximate, corridor, point, or unknown — and unknown is a correct answer, not a failure; (6) which other names on this list it overlapped, contained, succeeded, replaced, or competed with, each relation directional and evidenced. Four guardrails apply even at gazetteer depth, because they are the errors this subject invites. SoMa and South of Market are not the same name event — give them separate entries and do not assume their extents match at any date. South of the Slot is colloquial and social, not official, unless a specific official source establishes otherwise. The 100 Vara District is a land-survey and property-grid designation and must not be collapsed into SoMa. Rincon Hill's entry must distinguish its pre-1869 and post-1869 usage rather than averaging them, and The East Cut is a recent branding overlay unless a source establishes it as a neighborhood in its own right.

Context
Commissioned by DHK. This is one of two commissions drawn from the same project brief and the same attached extract. This one produces the gazetteer — every pilot name at even depth. Its sibling takes the five contested cases and the 1847-present event chronology at length. The two runs are independent and neither sees the other's output; where their source registries overlap, the agreement or disagreement between them is itself a check on both. The downstream consumer is a version-controlled research corpus — place records, time-bounded name events, source-specific geometry events, evidence assertions, and a relationship graph — that will later feed a map with a time slider and name search. This commission covers research substance only. The repository scaffolding, JSON schemas and Git governance described in the fuller project brief are being built separately and have been deliberately stripped from the attached extract; do not produce a project plan, a repository layout, or advice on how to run the research programme. The reason the corpus exists is that every readily available San Francisco neighborhood map presents current boundaries as though they were facts about the past, and the project's premise is that they are not. What is wanted is the material a human researcher would otherwise spend months assembling from the San Francisco History Center at SFPL, the David Rumsey Map Collection, Sanborn fire insurance volumes, city and business directories, historic block books, the Bancroft Library, the California Historical Society, San Francisco Planning's area plans and historic resource surveys, and the digitised newspaper record: a first pass at where the evidence is, what it says, and where it disagrees with itself. Web search is enabled on this run specifically because the deliverable's whole value is provenance — a recalled citation to an 1850s directory that turns out not to exist is worse than no citation at all — so treat the difference between a source you fetched and read today and a source you merely remember as the most important distinction in your output.

Constraints
Cover every name on the list before deepening any of them. An even gazetteer with thin entries beats four rich entries and nineteen omissions; if you are running short, compress prose, never drop a name. Do not manufacture exact start or end dates. Where evidence is partial, give a range and say what bounds it. Do not back-project present-day neighborhood boundaries into earlier periods; if the only boundary you can find is a modern one, say so and give no historical extent. Do not give a colloquial or cultural name a crisp parcel-level boundary — match the precision of the claim to the precision of the evidence. Preserve disagreement rather than resolving it: where two credible sources place a name differently, report both with their evidence and say which is better supported and why, and never let a secondary or retrospective account silently override contrary contemporaneous evidence. Distinguish throughout between a source contemporaneous with the usage — an 1899 Sanborn sheet labelling an area — and a later account describing that usage — a 2020 article saying what people called it in 1899; both are usable, they are not the same class of evidence, and the difference must be visible per claim rather than stated once as a caveat. Treat real-estate and branding language as evidence of what a developer or a business improvement district wanted, never as neutral historical fact. Every citation must carry enough to retrieve it: title, creator or publisher, date, repository, and identifier or URL. Mark each citation explicitly as FETCHED — you retrieved and read it during this run, with the URL as it read today — or RECALLED — it comes from training and you did not verify it. Do not blur the two. If you could not verify something you believe to be true, say so and place it under gaps rather than dressing it as a finding. A shorter output with clean provenance is worth more here than a comprehensive one that cannot be checked.

Output needs
A document that stands alone for a reader who has not seen this brief, in this order. (1) GAZETTEER: one entry per name in the task's list, in that order, each answering the six numbered questions in that order, with every dated claim carrying its source and locator inline and its FETCHED or RECALLED marker. Where a name yields nothing checkable, keep the entry and record it as insufficient evidence, naming what you looked for and where — an empty entry with a reason is a result, silence is not. Do not silently merge two names into one entry, however similar they look; if you judge them the same place, say so in both entries and give the evidence. (2) SOURCE REGISTRY: a table of every source you actually used, with title, creator, date, repository, identifier or stable URL, source class (primary cartographic, primary administrative, primary contemporaneous textual, secondary interpretive), FETCHED or RECALLED, and a one-line reliability note saying what that source can and cannot settle. (3) CONFLICT REGISTER: every place where sources disagree about a name's date, extent, or meaning, with both positions, their evidence, and your reading of which is stronger — and the ones you cannot adjudicate left explicitly unadjudicated. (4) GAPS AND NEXT MOVES: what a human researcher must go to a physical archive or a paywalled newspaper database to settle, named specifically enough to act on — which collection, which years, which map series — ranked by how much the gazetteer's integrity depends on it. (5) WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS ANSWER.
