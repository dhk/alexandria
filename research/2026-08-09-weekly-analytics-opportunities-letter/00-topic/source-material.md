# Research brief: a weekly opportunities letter for mid-career analysts

**Prepared for:** Dave Holmes-Kinsella
**Date:** 9 August 2026
**Working concept:** A free weekly letter curating opportunities shared
through Dave's network, intended to help mid-career data analysts and
analytics engineers find work suited to their careers and values.

## Executive conclusion

There is no shortage of data jobs, specialist job boards, alerts or career
content. The defensible opportunity is not to build a better database. It
is to publish a small, trusted letter that answers a more human question:

> Which opportunities reaching this particular network seem worth the
> attention of experienced analysts—and why?

The durable assets are the relationships that generate opportunities,
Dave's accumulated judgment about analytics work and organizations, the
trust of readers, and a permission-based email list. The individual
listings are perishable. The archive, software and formatting are
secondary.

Dave's taste should not be treated as an awkward subjective layer to
remove. It is the product. The important discipline is to make that taste
legible: distinguish facts from impressions, explain why something made
the cut, and never imply that an employer has been vetted when the
evidence is only a referral or a job description.

The strongest positioning is therefore:

> A free weekly note for mid-career data analysts and analytics
> engineers, featuring a small selection of opportunities shared through
> my network—with context on the work, the organization and why each one
> may be worth your attention.

## The market around the idea

The adjacent market falls into five recognizable models.

| Model | Representative examples | What it does well | Why it does not own this idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-volume general marketplace | LinkedIn, Indeed | Breadth, search, alerts and application flow | Volume creates noise; the relationship behind a listing is normally invisible |
| Role-specific job board | DataAnalyst.com, Analytics Engineering Jobs | Normalized filters, salaries, seniority and technical tags | Its unit of value is still a listing, not a trusted introduction or a curator's interpretation |
| Curated professional newsletter | Data Elixir | A habitual weekly relationship and a recognizable editorial voice | Primarily learning and industry content rather than network-sourced career opportunities |
| Culture- or mission-oriented marketplace | Welcome to the Jungle, Tech Jobs for Good | Makes company culture, mission or impact part of discovery | Uses platform-scale profiles and matching; values are categories rather than personal judgment |
| Peer job-search community | Never Search Alone, Locally Optimistic | Trust, mutual aid, accountability and practitioner community | Community is the primary service; neither is a concise, personally curated weekly opportunity letter for this specific audience |

### What the examples show

- **Role specificity is useful but insufficient.** Analytics Engineering
  Jobs already offers a curated board with seniority, salary and stack
  tags. DataAnalyst.com segments alerts by career level and publishes
  market insights. Competing on classification would mean recreating an
  existing commodity.
- **People want less noise, not another source of volume.** Welcome to
  the Jungle explicitly markets relevant matching and richer company
  context as an alternative to endless scrolling. Your version can
  deliver that benefit through human selection rather than a matching
  system.
- **Values can create a market boundary.** Tech Jobs for Good
  demonstrates that mission can be a primary discovery axis. Your letter
  need not be limited to nonprofits or social-impact employers, but it
  can take the quality and consequences of the work seriously.
- **Community makes a search emotionally and practically better.** Never
  Search Alone is built around the proposition that job seekers benefit
  from support, confidence and shared process. Locally Optimistic
  describes its analytics community as a practitioner "brain trust." Your
  letter can carry some of that humanity without becoming a coaching
  program or Slack community.

## What is durable

**1. A network that supplies opportunities before or alongside mass
distribution.** The strongest input is not a scraped posting. It is a
message from a former colleague, hiring manager, founder, investor or
practitioner saying, "We are looking for someone good." That carries
context and permits a useful follow-up question. This advantage is
durable only if the letter gives something back to the network:
thoughtful candidates, accurate representation of the role, gratitude,
and evidence that sharing with you is worthwhile.

**2. Judgment accumulated through doing and leading the work.** Job
titles in this field remain blurry. dbt's own career guidance notes that
analytics-engineering work is often hidden inside analyst or
data-engineer titles, so candidates must evaluate the work rather than
rely on the label (dbt Labs). Dave can interpret the actual shape of a
role: decision support, stakeholder partnership, metric design, modeling,
platform work, team building or dashboard production. That judgment
compounds. A taxonomy or scraper can be copied; decades of pattern
recognition cannot be copied quickly.

**3. Reader trust.** Trust will come from restraint:
- Publish five good opportunities rather than padding an issue to fifteen.
- Say exactly what is known and how it is known.
- Include a role because it may serve the reader, not because the
  employer paid.
- Correct errors visibly.
- Do not confuse "someone in my network shared this" with "I personally
  endorse this company."

**4. A direct audience relationship.** An opt-in email list is more
durable than dependence on LinkedIn distribution. LinkedIn can remain the
discovery and sharing layer, but the email letter should be the canonical
product. Each issue should also have a public web version so people can
forward a single opportunity or the whole edition.

**5. A recognizable editorial ritual.** Durability comes from a
repeatable promise and cadence, not ever-expanding features. The format
should be small enough to publish during a thin week and useful enough
that readers open it even when they are not actively searching.

## Where Dave's taste belongs

Taste is not "I like this company." It is a consistent set of questions
applied to incomplete evidence.

### The proposed editorial lens

For each opportunity, consider:

1. **Is this consequential analytics work?** Will the person influence a
   real decision, product or operating process?
2. **Is the role legible?** Can a candidate tell what success looks like,
   or is the description an undifferentiated wish list?
3. **Does the organization understand the craft?** Are analytics,
   analytics engineering and data engineering treated as distinct but
   collaborative capabilities?
4. **Is there room for a mid-career person to exercise judgment?** The
   audience is not looking merely to execute tickets or produce
   dashboards on demand.
5. **What human signals are present?** Who shared it? What do they know?
   Is the hiring manager identifiable? Is there evidence of respect for
   candidates and colleagues?
6. **What values are implicated?** What does the company make possible,
   for whom, and at whose expense? Are there material concerns the reader
   should investigate?
7. **Is the bargain plausible?** Where available: compensation, location,
   flexibility, scope, seniority and expectations.

The letter should not assign a synthetic score. Scores suggest a level of
comparable evidence that will rarely exist. A concise editorial note is
more honest and more distinctly Dave.

### A useful annotation vocabulary

Each item can state:
- **Why it caught my eye** — Dave's interpretation.
- **What I know** — facts supplied by the posting or network contact.
- **What I would ask** — the unresolved question a serious candidate
  should investigate.
- **Connection available** — only when Dave has permission and a
  realistic path to introduce someone.

This makes taste visible without turning it into unsupported endorsement.

## How the letter can differentiate

### The core difference

Most job products optimize one or more of volume, matching, application
speed or employer reach. This letter should optimize attention, context
and trust.

| Common product promise | Dave's alternative |
|---|---|
| "Thousands of current jobs" | "A few opportunities that reached people I know" |
| "Matched to your profile" | "Interpreted for experienced analytics practitioners" |
| "Apply faster" | "Decide more intelligently where to invest effort" |
| "Values-based employers" | "Concrete questions about the work, organization and consequences" |
| "Career content" | "One useful observation arising from this week's roles" |
| "Build your personal brand" | "Help good people and good teams find one another" |

### A position worth owning

**The network note for thoughtful analytics careers.**

This territory joins three things that competitors generally separate:

1. A defined craft community: mid-career analysts and analytics
   engineers.
2. Opportunities that arrive through human relationships.
3. Editorial judgment about both career fit and values.

The word **network** should mean provenance, not exclusivity. Some
opportunities will already be public. Their value is that a trusted
person surfaced them and may be able to add context—not that the letter
possesses secret jobs.

## Recommended product shape

### Weekly issue

1. **A short opening note** — One observation from the week's
   conversations or roles.
2. **Three to eight opportunities** — Each includes role, organization,
   location/remote status, compensation when known, source link, why it
   caught Dave's eye, what is known, and what to ask.
3. **One network request** — For example: a hiring manager seeking a
   particular background, or a community member asking for an
   introduction. Publish only with permission.
4. **One durable resource** — A practical job-search tool, example,
   question or piece of advice—not a generic link roundup.
5. **Invitation to contribute** — "Send me a role you would be
   comfortable putting in front of people you respect."

### Explicit exclusions

- No attempt to be comprehensive.
- No scraped or automatically generated filler.
- No entry-level focus.
- No generic data-science or software-engineering roles unless the actual
  work fits the audience.
- No paid placement during the trust-building phase.
- No guarantee that a listed organization is good or that a reader will
  receive an introduction.
- No private community, job tracker or application automation at launch.

## The risks

**Network exhaustion.** A personal network may not yield enough relevant
roles every week. The remedy is not scraping. Allow short editions,
broaden contribution through trusted second-degree sources, and
occasionally publish a market or craft note with no obligation to hit a
listing quota.

**Ambiguous endorsement.** Readers may infer that Dave has vetted every
employer. Use a consistent provenance line and a standing disclaimer:
curation means "worth investigating," not "guaranteed good."

**Values becoming vague or moralizing.** "Aligned with your values" is
personal; Dave cannot determine alignment for everyone. The editorial job
is to surface evidence and questions, not issue moral certification.

**Introductions becoming unmanageable.** Do not promise an introduction
for every role or subscriber. State clearly when an introduction may be
possible, request a concise expression of interest, and preserve the
contact's permission and attention.

**Becoming a job board.** Structured listings, employer intake and
sponsorship can slowly displace the voice and network. The governing test
should be: **Would this change improve the reader's judgment, or merely
increase inventory?**

**Serving two audiences.** The reader is the primary customer even though
the letter is free. Employers and network contacts are contributors. If
their interests conflict, protect reader trust.

## Six-week pilot

The concept can be tested manually before naming, branding or building
software.

**Before issue one**
- Write a one-paragraph charter and the editorial lens.
- Ask 15–20 trusted people to forward appropriate roles.
- Establish a simple submission format: role link, how the sender knows
  the team, why it may be good, and whether an introduction is possible.
- Invite an initial group of mid-career analysts and analytics engineers
  directly.

**During the pilot**
- Publish on the same day each week.
- Cap each issue at eight roles; publish fewer when necessary.
- Personally annotate every role.
- Track replies, forwards, contributed roles, requests for introductions
  and reported outcomes.
- Ask one different feedback question at the end of each issue.

### Evidence of promise

Subscriber count is a weak early metric. More meaningful signals are:
- Hiring managers and practitioners voluntarily send roles again.
- Readers forward the letter to people they respect.
- Readers reply with specific questions or context.
- Introductions lead to credible conversations.
- People continue reading when they are not actively applying.
- The letter sharpens Dave's own relationships and point of view.

### Decision after six weeks

Continue if the supply of roles and reader engagement both appear
relational rather than promotional. Adjust the cadence if quality supply
is intermittent. Stop or reposition if most inventory must be scraped, or
if the work feels like maintaining a board rather than exercising
judgment and strengthening a community.

## Recommended working language

**One-sentence promise**
> Every week, I share a small selection of opportunities that have
> reached me through my network—hopefully helping mid-career data
> analysts and analytics engineers find work suited to their careers and
> values.

**Slightly more specific version**
> A free weekly note for mid-career data analysts and analytics
> engineers: a few opportunities shared through my network, along with
> context on the work, the organization and the questions I think are
> worth asking.

**Submission invitation**
> Hiring—or know someone who is? Send me an analytics role you would be
> comfortable putting in front of people you respect. Tell me how you
> know the team, why the work matters and whether you may be able to
> make an introduction.

## Bottom line

Do not compete with job boards on inventory or software. Do not compete
with career newsletters on content volume. Build a trusted weekly
practice around a simple act: **people Dave respects send him
opportunities; Dave applies informed taste; people doing thoughtful
analytics work receive a useful signal.**

If that loop becomes habitual, the audience is not merely a mailing
list. It becomes the tribe the project is intended to focus and serve.
