## Comparative Assessment Report

### Overview

All three outputs reach the same headline verdict—no single writing technique serves all organizational genres—and share a common analytical architecture: a demand matrix (genres × qualities), a supply matrix (techniques × qualities), and a selection procedure that matches them. The level of agreement on individual claims is high, with most substantive positions corroborated across all three. Disagreements are mostly of degree, emphasis, or granularity rather than direction.

### Strong Points of Convergence

**Genre-first selection** is unanimous. All three argue that the correct organizing principle is to diagnose the genre's demand profile before choosing a technique, and that defaulting to any single house style is an error. The framing is nearly identical across outputs.

**Plainspoken as chat-only** is unanimous and unusually precise: all three cite the skill's own documentation as the evidence that it is scoped to conversational register and should not govern durable documents.

**Hemingway/readability formulas** are uniformly rated the weakest technique on evidence grounds. One output calls it the weakest on the list outright; the others reach the same conclusion through their matrix scores and evidence audits. The specific mechanism—grade-level scoring penalizes qualifying clauses that carry calibrated uncertainty—is stated explicitly by two outputs and implied by the third.

**Sequential rather than simultaneous application** of techniques is unanimous: structure first, then sentence-level, then word-level passes.

**Blameless postmortem as a genre-specific convention** that actively damages accountability in other genres is unanimous, with all three noting the deliberate individual-agency suppression as a feature in its home genre and a defect elsewhere.

**Incident notification as the most underserved genre** is unanimous. All three identify the same gap: no available technique simultaneously handles extreme brevity, real-time confidence calibration, empathy, and accessibility under moving facts.

**STE/ste damaging persuasive force** is unanimous, with all three disqualifying it for dissent and argumentation-heavy genres.

**Humanize's default Pass 1 stripping calibrated hedges** is unanimous, with all three noting that science/paper overrides restore calibration but the default mode does not.

### Notable Differences

**Postmortem accountability scoring**: One output scores postmortem accountability as a low positive (3 on a 0–3 scale), treating blameless as a cultural choice. A second output explicitly assigns a negative score (−1) to accountability in the postmortem column, arguing the genre actively does not want individual accountability language. The third uses a footnote to explain the same tension. The second output's explicit negative cell is the most analytically precise treatment.

**STE and accountability**: One output notes that STE's imperative-only and agentless constructions damage accountability, giving it a negative supply score on that dimension. The other two are less explicit on this specific mechanism, though they reach compatible conclusions about STE's limits in decision records.

**Toulmin + Minto as complementary**: One output states this explicitly and clearly—Pyramid places the claim, Toulmin describes what goes beneath it. The other two imply the same combination in their worked examples but do not articulate the relationship as a principle.

**STE vs. plain language incompatibility**: One output explicitly states they should not be combined within a single document because STE's controlled vocabulary is stricter. The others recommend choosing one or the other but do not explain the incompatibility mechanism.

**Dissent as underserved**: Two outputs identify dissent that must be both persuasive and durable inside a hierarchy as a second major gap, specifically because no technique handles the interpersonal/empathy load. One output treats this gap as secondary to the incident notification gap.

**Evidence classification rigor**: The outputs vary in how formally they classify evidence. One uses a four-category system (experimental, institutional, practitioner, folklore) applied per claim. Another uses a similar system but applies it per technique. The third uses inline markers throughout the matrices. All reach the same conclusion: institutional adoption and practitioner consensus dominate; experimental evidence is thin across the board; plain language and Toulmin have the strongest experimental support.

**humanize helping accountability**: One output makes the specific claim that humanize's "attribution to nobody" flag helps accountability by catching agentless constructions like "studies show" without citation. The other two do not make this specific positive claim for humanize on the accountability dimension.

### What This Run Does Not Establish

- **Whether any of these techniques actually improve organizational outcomes** (decision quality, error rates, time-to-action, learning) in controlled conditions. All three outputs acknowledge this gap explicitly. The evidence base is dominated by institutional adoption, not experiments.
- **Whether the matrix scores are calibrated correctly** relative to each other. The outputs agree on direction (positive/negative) for nearly all cells but differ on magnitude. No output provides a method for resolving disagreements about cell values.
- **Whether the ten genres and ten considerations are the right decomposition** for any specific organization's actual writing portfolio. All three note that a narrower target (e.g., SREs, researchers, PMs) would collapse the matrix onto a subset and sharpen recommendations.
- **Whether the humanize skill's AI-tells catalog changes reader trust or comprehension**, as opposed to merely reducing suspicion of AI origin. All three flag this as untested.
- **Whether blameless language can be reconciled with durable individual accountability** without chilling honest reporting—or whether visible agency in postmortems reduces learning. This is identified as an open empirical question.
- **Whether the repo skills (ste, plainspoken, humanize) perform as described** when applied at scale to the ten genres by real users. All three treat them as promising but unvalidated heuristics whose efficacy inherits from the underlying techniques rather than being independently established.
- **Whether a calibration vocabulary standard for moving-facts incident notifications** (confirmed / suspected / unknown / ruled out) would close the identified gap, or whether organizational and legal constraints would prevent its adoption.
